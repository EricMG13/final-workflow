### Credit view

SM Energy's credit standing is sound and improving, but it depends on oil prices and on a newly merged earnings base. The strongest support is balance-sheet strength: net debt of USD 6,014 million is 1.42x trailing adjusted EBITDAX, the revolver is undrawn, liquidity is USD 2,940 million, and no note matures before mid-2028. The weakest point is that trailing earnings blend the old and merged company, so leverage is not on a clean pro forma basis, and covenant terms, ratings and legal documents are not in the sources. The outcome will be decided by oil prices and basis through 2027, by whether free cash flow keeps funding both debt reduction and shareholder returns, and by how the USD 3,956 million of notes due 2028 to 2031 is managed.

### Key findings

SM Energy cut net debt by USD 1,339 million in two quarters, from USD 7,353 million at 2026-03-31 to USD 6,014 million at 2026-09-30, and trailing leverage fell from 2.79x to 1.42x over the same dates [CP-1: leverage and net debt tables; calculation 3]. The mechanism was asset-sale proceeds of USD 894 million and operating cash flow, which together redeemed the 2026 and 2027 notes at par while the revolver stayed undrawn at each quarter end [CP-1A: transaction summary]. The ratio improved partly because trailing earnings gradually absorbed more of the acquired business, so some of the decline is a perimeter effect and not debt repayment alone. The Civitas merger closed on 2026-01-30 and was equity-funded, so SM Energy's creditors face a larger company with the same unsecured senior notes and a secured revolver, not a leveraged buyout.

Liquidity is strong: cash of USD 442 million plus USD 2,498 million of undrawn revolver capacity gives USD 2,940 million, while the borrowing base of USD 5,000 million is twice the USD 2,500 million of commitments, so availability is capped by lender commitments, not by reserves [CP-1: liquidity]. The maturity ladder is USD 856 million in 2028, USD 750 million in 2029, USD 1,000 million in 2030, USD 1,350 million in 2031 and USD 2,500 million across 2032 to 2034, so the wall begins in mid-2028 and totals USD 3,956 million across 2028 to 2031 [CP-1: debt maturities]. Cash generation covers the dividends: operating cash flow less capital expenditures was USD 845 million in the nine months against USD 187 million of dividends and USD 166 million of share repurchases, leaving USD 492 million before asset-sale proceeds [CP-1: calculation 7; this module's calculation 4].

The cost of the debt stack is the main structural concern. The notes carry a weighted coupon of 7.90 percent, or USD 510.1 million of coupons a year, and the notes assumed from Civitas, USD 3,556 million at 8.375 to 9.625 percent, are the most expensive layer, with a weighted coupon of 8.85 percent [CP-1: debt schedule; calculation 2]. Trailing cash interest cover of 12.73x blends the lighter pre-merger interest burden with the post-merger burden; against run-rate coupons the cover is 8.28x, which is the better guide to the current stack. Whether the high-coupon notes can be called or refinanced early depends on indenture terms that are not supplied.

Earnings carry commodity risk. Oil was about 84 percent of Q3 2026 production revenue and the Permian Basin about half of it [CP-1A: company and operating model]. Hedges are swaps and collars through the second quarter of 2028 with prices below recent realized oil prices, so they cost cash now and cap upside, and the net derivative position is a liability of USD 460 million [CP-1A: hedging]. Operating issues also matter: oil production guidance was cut for Permian downtime on acquired assets and Uinta takeaway constraints, and lease operating cost per barrel of oil equivalent has risen every quarter since Q4 2025 [CP-1A: volume risks, cost structure]. Claims outside the debt figure include delivery commitments with up to USD 205 million of deficiency payments if nothing were delivered, liquidated damages of up to USD 64 million under a drilling agreement, and a drilling commitment management expects to be excused [CP-1: claims outside the debt figure].

Shareholder returns and governance show no creditor-adverse behaviour in the documents: the merger was paid in shares, there is no sponsor, and no debt-funded distribution, drop-down or priming appears [CP-1A: behaviour table]. Management targets about 20 percent of free cash flow after dividends for repurchases, which competes with deleveraging, though management says its leverage trajectory is part of that allocation. Legal capacity to distribute cash or add priority debt cannot be tested because the indentures and the credit agreement are not supplied.

### Strengths and weaknesses

Strengths. SM Energy has low net leverage of 1.42x on the filed trailing basis and has shown it will use asset sales and cash flow to retire debt, with USD 1,236 million of notes redeemed at par since March from sale proceeds and cash flow (400, 419 and 417). It has no note maturity before mid-2028 and USD 2,940 million of liquidity, including a revolver that was undrawn at every 2026 quarter end. It has scale and spread across four basins, with 433.1 thousand barrels of oil equivalent a day in Q3 2026 with the Permian Basin about half of revenue. Operating cash flow after capital spending funds the dividend several times over. The merger was equity-funded, and lenders reaffirmed a borrowing base of USD 5,000 million after the quarter.

Weaknesses. SM Energy remains a price taker with revenue about 84 percent oil, hedges below recent realized prices and a net derivative liability of USD 460 million. Its trailing credit metrics mix standalone and merged periods and are not covenant metrics. Interest cost is higher after the merger, as the assumed notes carry coupons of 8.375 to 9.625 percent and will keep interest above the pre-merger run-rate until they are retired or refinanced. Integration and operating execution still carry risk, with oil guidance lowered, rising unit lease operating costs and acquired-asset downtime. Off-debt commitments exist but are unquantified in part. Shareholder returns continue alongside deleveraging. The documentation behind the credit, including covenants, guarantors, indentures, ratings and a post-merger reserve base, is not in the sources.

## Calculations

All inputs come from the CP-1 and CP-1A handoffs; amounts in USD millions.

1. Net debt reduction between 2026-03-31 and 2026-09-30 = 7,353 - 6,014 = 1,339.
2. Notes assumed from Civitas and still outstanding = 456 (8.375 percent) + 1,000 (8.625 percent) + 1,350 (8.75 percent) + 750 (9.625 percent) = 3,556. Annual coupons = 38.2 + 86.3 + 118.1 + 72.2 = 314.8 (from 456 x 8.375 percent = 38.19, 1,000 x 8.625 percent = 86.25, 1,350 x 8.75 percent = 118.125, 750 x 9.625 percent = 72.1875, sum 314.75). Weighted coupon = 314.75 / 3,556 = 8.85 percent.
3. Maturities 2028 to 2031 = 856 + 750 + 1,000 + 1,350 = 3,956. Maturities 2032 to 2034 = 750 + 750 + 1,000 = 2,500. Total = 6,456, equal to principal.
4. Cash after dividends and repurchases, nine months 2026 = operating cash flow less capital expenditures 845 - dividends paid 187 - cash repurchases of common stock 166 = 492. Inputs from the CP-1 financial table and CP-1A shareholder return rows (Q3 10-Q page:8 for the cash repurchase figure, a pinned-source locator added for the trace).
5. Redemptions at par since March: 400 (2026-05-11) + 419 (2026-06-01) + 417 (2026-09-04) = 1,236, matching the CP-1 cross-check of 924 + 1,236 = 2,160 cash paid to repurchase notes.
6. Leverage and cover figures are those of the CP-1 handoff: 1.42x net debt to trailing adjusted EBITDAX, 1.53x total debt, 12.73x trailing EBITDAX to trailing cash interest, 8.28x trailing EBITDAX to run-rate coupons of 510.1 per year (weighted coupon 7.90 percent).
